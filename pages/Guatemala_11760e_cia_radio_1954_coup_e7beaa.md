## Introduction

In June 1954, a small US-backed force crossed into [Guatemala]({{ 'how-false-stories-changed-guatemalas/' | relative_url }}) with little chance of winning a conventional war. Yet within days President Jacobo Árbenz's government was collapsing. One of the most important reasons was not military strength but a carefully constructed illusion: a clandestine radio station that convinced many listeners that a powerful rebel army was sweeping across the country. The station, known as the Voice of Liberation (La Voz de la Liberación), was a CIA psychological warfare project created as part of Operation PBSUCCESS. It broadcast invented victories, exaggerated defections and fabricated reports of rebel advances, helping turn a minor invasion into what seemed like an unstoppable national uprising.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1954_Guatemalan_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1954 Guatemalan coup d&#x27;état</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 8, 2026 — The most wide-reaching psychological weapon was the radio station Voice of Liberation, La Voz de la Liberación. ## Why the Rebel Force Needed an Illusion

The military reality facing the anti-Árbenz rebels was stark. Carlos Castillo Armas entered Guatemala with only a few hundred fighters. The Guatemalan army was much larger and, on paper, capable of defeating the invasion. CIA planners understood that battlefield victories alone were unlikely to bring down the government. Instead, they aimed to undermine confidence within the army and among political elites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guatemalan_Revolution" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Guatemalan Revolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guatemalan Revolution</span></span></span>

Psychological warfare had been built into the operation from the beginning. Declassified planning documents show that propaganda and political action were considered essential components of the campaign rather than secondary support activities. CIA officials believed that if key officers concluded that Árbenz was doomed, they might refuse to defend him even if the rebels remained militarily weak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54Guat/d51" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the HistorianForeign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, GuatemalaAs a result of this, existing CIA assets personnelwise...</span></span></span>

The radio station became the centrepiece of that strategy because radio was one of the fastest ways to reach large audiences in 1950s Guatemala. News often travelled through broadcasts, rumours and word of mouth. If listeners could be persuaded that rebel columns were appearing everywhere at once, the perception of momentum could become more important than the actual balance of forces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/ciacase/E55-56.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive2.gwu.edu">[gwu.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive2.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security ArchiveE55-56The Voice of Liberation. ## How the Voice of Liberation Fabricated Victory

The Voice of Liberation began broadcasting on 1 May 1954. It claimed to be operating from inside Guatemala, supposedly from territory controlled by anti-government forces deep in the countryside. In reality, the operation was organised by the CIA and its collaborators. David Atlee Phillips, who later became a well-known CIA officer, played a central role in directing the broadcasts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1954_Guatemalan_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1954 Guatemalan coup d&#x27;état</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 8, 2026 — The most wide-reaching psychological weapon was the radio station Voice of Liberation, La Voz de la Liberación. E. Howard...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 8, 2026</span></span></span>

The station's power came from its presentation. Rather than sounding like a foreign propaganda outlet, it imitated the style of a local revolutionary broadcaster. Announcers delivered urgent bulletins, battlefield updates and reports from supposed rebel commanders. The broadcasts created the impression that listeners were hearing information from a growing resistance movement already active across the country.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/ciacase/E55-56.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive2.gwu.edu">[gwu.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive2.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security ArchiveE55-56The Voice of Liberation. Many of the station's most dramatic reports were exaggerated or entirely fabricated. It announced victories that had not occurred, described rebel units that did not exist and suggested that government forces were defecting in large numbers. The station repeatedly portrayed Castillo Armas's forces as far larger and more successful than they really were.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/ciacase/E55-56.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive2.gwu.edu">[gwu.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive2.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security ArchiveE55-56The Voice of Liberation. Several techniques made the deception persuasive:

* **False location claims:** The station insisted it was broadcasting from within Guatemala, giving the impression of an established insurgent network.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1954_Guatemalan_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1954 Guatemalan coup d&#x27;état</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 8, 2026 — The most wide-reaching psychological weapon was the radio station Voice of Liberation, La Voz de la Liberación. E. Howard...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 8, 2026</span></span></span>
* **Invented battlefield reports:** Announcers described rebel advances and victories regardless of the actual military situation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/ciacase/E55-56.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive2.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive2.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security ArchiveE55-56The Voice of Liberation. As Guatemalans turned on their short-wave radios on the morning of 1 May 1954, th...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1954</span></span></span>
* **Claims of mass support:** Broadcasts suggested that civilians, soldiers and local leaders were rapidly joining the rebellion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1954_Guatemalan_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1954 Guatemalan coup d&#x27;état</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 8, 2026 — The most wide-reaching psychological weapon was the radio station Voice of Liberation, La Voz de la Liberación. E. Howard...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 8, 2026</span></span></span>
* **Constant repetition:** Messages were repeated frequently, helping rumours circulate beyond the radio audience through conversations and local gossip.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radiosurvivor.com/2011/03/how-the-cia-used-radio-in-the-1954-guatemala-coup/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radiosurvivor.com">[Radio Survivor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radiosurvivor.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">how the cia used radio in the 1954 guatemala coup</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">how the cia used radio in the 1954 guatemala coup</span></span></span>

The operation also benefited from circumstances beyond CIA planning. At one point Guatemala's state radio service experienced a scheduled interruption, reducing competition for listeners and making the clandestine station more prominent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radiosurvivor.com/2011/03/how-the-cia-used-radio-in-the-1954-guatemala-coup/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radiosurvivor.com">[Radio Survivor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radiosurvivor.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">how the cia used radio in the 1954 guatemala coup</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">how the cia used radio in the 1954 guatemala coup</span></span></span>

## Why So Many People Believed It

The Voice of Liberation did not succeed because every listener accepted every claim. Its effectiveness came from uncertainty. During a fast-moving political crisis, people often lacked reliable information. Few Guatemalans could independently verify reports from distant towns or military zones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/ciacase/E55-56.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive2.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive2.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security ArchiveE55-56The Voice of Liberation. As Guatemalans turned on their short-wave radios on the morning of 1 May 1954, th...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1954</span></span></span>

The broadcasts were reinforced by other elements of the coup campaign. Aircraft associated with the operation carried out limited attacks that created a sense of danger out of proportion to their military impact. Leaflets circulated. Rumours spread through cities and army barracks. The radio station then tied these events together into a narrative of inevitable rebel victory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://text-message.blogs.archives.gov/2012/02/13/the-cia-in-guatemala/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: text-message.blogs.archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">text-message.blogs.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Text Message The CIA in Guatemala</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Text MessageThe CIA in Guatemala - The Text MessageFeb 13, 2012 — CIA methods employed in their Guatemalan operations included misinf...</span></span></span>

Foreign journalists occasionally repeated information originating from the broadcasts, allowing claims from a covert propaganda outlet to acquire additional credibility. Once stories moved beyond the station itself and entered wider reporting networks, distinguishing fact from fiction became even more difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radiosurvivor.com/2011/03/how-the-cia-used-radio-in-the-1954-guatemala-coup/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radiosurvivor.com">[Radio Survivor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radiosurvivor.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">how the cia used radio in the 1954 guatemala coup</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">how the cia used radio in the 1954 guatemala coup</span></span></span>

Most importantly, the station targeted decision-makers as much as ordinary listeners. Senior military officers did not need to believe every report. They only needed to fear that other officers believed them. The resulting uncertainty encouraged hesitation and weakened confidence in the government's ability to survive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitalcommons.denison.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1162&amp;context=studentscholarship" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitalcommons.denison.edu">[digitalcommons.denison.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitalcommons.denison.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on denison.edu.</span></span></span>

## What the Deception Changed — and What It Did Not

The Voice of Liberation was influential, but it did not single-handedly overthrow Árbenz. The coup also involved diplomatic pressure, covert political operations, military actions, economic pressure and efforts to influence members of the Guatemalan armed forces. Árbenz's resignation resulted from the interaction of these factors rather than from radio broadcasts alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54Guat/d287" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[state.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on state.gov.</span></span></span>

What the station clearly changed was the perceived balance of power. It helped transform a small rebel force into what appeared to be a broad national movement. By magnifying the rebels' strength and minimising their setbacks, the broadcasts contributed to a climate in which resistance seemed increasingly pointless.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guatemalan_Revolution" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Guatemalan Revolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guatemalan Revolution</span></span></span>

The deception therefore worked less as a substitute for military action than as a force multiplier. It exploited fear, uncertainty and the difficulty of verifying information during a crisis. The station's success demonstrated that perceptions could become strategically important even when they diverged sharply from reality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/ciacase/E55-56.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive2.gwu.edu">[gwu.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive2.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security ArchiveE55-56The Voice of Liberation. The true nature of the operation became clearer over time through memoirs, historical research and the release of declassified US government documents. ## Why the Radio Deception Still Matters

The Voice of Liberation remains relevant because it anticipated techniques that later became common in information warfare. The operation showed how a relatively small media outlet could shape political events if it appeared credible, repeated its message consistently and exploited existing fears.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dialnet.unirioja.es/servlet/articulo?codigo=10075425" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dialnet.unirioja.es">[Dialnet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dialnet.unirioja.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on unirioja.es.</span></span></span>

For Guatemala, the episode stands as a reminder that one of the country's most consequential political turning points was influenced not only by soldiers and weapons but also by a manufactured narrative. The coup's radio campaign demonstrated that a fake army did not have to exist physically in order to affect real decisions.
