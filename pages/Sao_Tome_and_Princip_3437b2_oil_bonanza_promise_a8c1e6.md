## Introduction

Did São Tomé ever have an oil fortune? The short answer is no. What the country had was a powerful promise of future wealth. From the late 1990s onward, geologists identified offshore areas that might contain significant petroleum reserves. As international oil companies, investors and politicians focused on the Gulf of Guinea, many public discussions began to treat possible discoveries as though they were almost guaranteed riches. Yet commercially recoverable oil was never proven on the scale that had been anticipated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scienceopen.com/hosted-document?doi=10.1080%2F03056240802411156" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scienceopen.com">[scienceopen.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scienceopen.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hosted document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hope &amp; Oil: Expectations in São Tomé e Príncipeby G Weszkalnys · 2008 · Cited by 87 — Similarly, a relatively non-violent coup...</span></span></span>

This matters because the expectation of oil wealth had political effects long before any oil was produced. Anticipated revenues shaped elections, government disputes, contract negotiations, corruption allegations and even interpretations of the country's 2003 coup attempt. In São Tomé and Príncipe, one of the most influential “too-good-to-be-true” stories was not a fabricated treasure or forged artefact, but the belief that offshore oil would soon transform a poor island nation into a petroleum state.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scienceopen.com/hosted-document?doi=10.1080%2F03056240802411156" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scienceopen.com">[scienceopen.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scienceopen.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hosted document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hope &amp; Oil: Expectations in São Tomé e Príncipeby G Weszkalnys · 2008 · Cited by 87 — Similarly, a relatively non-violent coup...</span></span></span>

## From geological possibility to public certainty

The origins of the oil story were not fraudulent. Offshore geological surveys suggested that waters around São Tomé and Príncipe might contain hydrocarbons similar to those found in neighbouring parts of the Gulf of Guinea. The country and Nigeria created a Joint Development Zone (JDZ) in 2001 to manage exploration in a disputed offshore area and share potential revenues.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nstpjda.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nstpjda.org">[NSTPJDA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nstpjda.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSTPJDA – Nigeria – São Tomé and Príncipe Joint...The agreement is expected to end the border dispute that has blocked deep water...</span></span></span>

The problem was the growing gap between possibility and certainty. Exploration rights attracted enormous attention. In 2003, companies bid hundreds of millions of dollars for access to offshore blocks, creating headlines that implied vast wealth was just around the corner. Expectations rose faster than evidence. Signature bonuses paid for exploration rights were real money, but they were often interpreted by the public as proof that major oil discoveries had already been made.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenewhumanitarian.org/report/46944/nigeria-sao-tome-and-principe-oil-companies-bid-over-500m-offshore-exploration-rights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenewhumanitarian.org">[The New Humanitarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenewhumanitarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New HumanitarianOil companies bid over $500m for offshore exploration rightsOctober 28, 2003 — 28 Oct 2003 — US and Nigerian oil comp...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 28, 2003</span></span></span>

Anthropologist Gisa Weszkalnys later described São Tomé and Príncipe as a place living with “hope and oil” before an oil economy actually existed. Citizens, politicians and foreign investors debated how future petroleum wealth would be spent, distributed and protected even though commercial production remained hypothetical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scienceopen.com/hosted-document?doi=10.1080%2F03056240802411156" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scienceopen.com">[ScienceOpen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scienceopen.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hosted document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hope &amp; Oil: Expectations in São Tomé e Príncipeby G Weszkalnys · 2008 · Cited by 87 — Similarly, a relatively non-violent coup...</span></span></span>

This created a peculiar situation: an economy and political culture increasingly influenced by a resource that had not yet been found in commercially viable quantities. The expectation itself became a political force.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scienceopen.com/hosted-document?doi=10.1080%2F03056240802411156" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scienceopen.com">[ScienceOpen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scienceopen.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hosted document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hope &amp; Oil: Expectations in São Tomé e Príncipeby G Weszkalnys · 2008 · Cited by 87 — Similarly, a relatively non-violent coup...</span></span></span>

## Contracts, corruption claims and political competition

Once oil was treated as inevitable, control over future revenues became politically valuable. Disputes emerged over exploration agreements, licensing rounds and relationships with foreign companies.

Several early contracts became controversial. Critics argued that some agreements granted excessive advantages to outside interests or were negotiated without sufficient transparency. Political leaders disagreed publicly about the terms of certain deals, and some agreements were challenged or renegotiated after concerns that they were not in the country's best interests.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://afsaap.org.au/assets/seibert.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: afsaap.org.au">[African Studies Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">afsaap.org.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on afsaap.org.au.</span></span></span>

The stakes increased as licensing rounds awarded offshore blocks. In 2005, São Tomé's Attorney General reported “serious flaws” in the allocation of some exploration contracts. The investigation questioned whether certain companies possessed the technical expertise and financial capacity expected for deep-water exploration. The findings reinforced public suspicion that political connections sometimes mattered more than industry competence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenewhumanitarian.org/news/2005/12/15/attorney-general-finds-%E2%80%98serious-flaws%E2%80%99-award-oil-exploration-contracts" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenewhumanitarian.org">[The New Humanitarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenewhumanitarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Humanitarian Attorney General finds &#x27;serious flaws&#x27; in the award of oil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New Humanitarian Attorney General finds &#x27;serious flaws&#x27; in the award of oil</span></span></span>

The prospect of future oil wealth also became entangled with broader concerns about corruption. Opponents accused governments of positioning allies to benefit from anticipated petroleum revenues. Even before production existed, debates over who would control future wealth intensified competition between political factions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenewhumanitarian.org/news/2005/12/15/attorney-general-finds-%E2%80%98serious-flaws%E2%80%99-award-oil-exploration-contracts" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenewhumanitarian.org">[The New Humanitarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenewhumanitarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Humanitarian Attorney General finds &#x27;serious flaws&#x27; in the award of oil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New Humanitarian Attorney General finds &#x27;serious flaws&#x27; in the award of oil</span></span></span>

International organisations recognised these risks early. Rather than waiting for oil production to begin, São Tomé and Príncipe adopted an Oil Revenue Management Law designed to promote transparency and limit misuse of future revenues. The legislation was widely praised because it attempted to create safeguards before the money arrived, reflecting awareness that expectations alone could destabilise politics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/001/2006/183/article-A001-en.xml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: elibrary.imf.org">[IMF eLibrary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">elibrary.imf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on imf.org.</span></span></span>

### Oil and the 2003 coup atmosphere

The country's brief 2003 coup occurred before any major discovery, but oil formed part of the political background. Contemporary observers and later analysts noted that anticipated petroleum wealth heightened both domestic political tensions and international interest in the islands. Coup leaders cited corruption and inequality among their grievances, while negotiations restoring civilian rule included commitments concerning transparent management of future oil revenues.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://issafrica.org/research/papers/coup-detat-in-sao-tome-and-principe-domestic-causes-the-role-of-oil-and-former-buffalo-battalion-soldiers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: issafrica.org">[ISS Africa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">issafrica.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISS AfricaCoup d&#x27;etat in Sao Tome and Principe: Domestic causes...2 Nov 2003 — This article draws attention to the political development...</span></span></span>

It would be misleading to claim that oil caused the coup. The event had multiple domestic causes, including military grievances and constitutional disputes. However, expectations surrounding petroleum wealth clearly influenced how political actors understood the country's future and what was at stake in controlling the state.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://issafrica.org/research/papers/coup-detat-in-sao-tome-and-principe-domestic-causes-the-role-of-oil-and-former-buffalo-battalion-soldiers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: issafrica.org">[ISS Africa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">issafrica.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISS AfricaCoup d&#x27;etat in Sao Tome and Principe: Domestic causes...2 Nov 2003 — This article draws attention to the political development...</span></span></span>

## Why exploration never guaranteed national wealth

The central misconception was the assumption that promising geology automatically meant national prosperity.

Oil exploration is highly uncertain. A company may spend millions of dollars investigating a region and still fail to find commercially recoverable reserves. Even when hydrocarbons are discovered, the quantities may be too small, too deep, too technically difficult or too expensive to develop profitably.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/4ff961a6-179e-51d7-b674-f1a02b22b120/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openknowledge.worldbank.org">[Open Knowledge Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openknowledge.worldbank.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Of the major oil companies: ExxonMobil, Chevron, and Total have each taken turns in Block 1 of the JDZ but haven&#x27;t succeeded...</span></span></span>

That is largely what happened in São Tomé and Príncipe. Exploration produced indications of hydrocarbons and some discoveries, but follow-up drilling failed to confirm the kind of giant fields that early enthusiasts had imagined. Major international companies entered and later exited key blocks after results proved disappointing. ExxonMobil, Chevron and Total all participated in exploration efforts without establishing the transformational petroleum industry many had expected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/4ff961a6-179e-51d7-b674-f1a02b22b120/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openknowledge.worldbank.org">[Open Knowledge Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openknowledge.worldbank.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Of the major oil companies: ExxonMobil, Chevron, and Total have each taken turns in Block 1 of the JDZ but haven&#x27;t succeeded...</span></span></span>

The Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative continues to describe the country's petroleum sector as nascent and notes that no significant oil discoveries have yet been made. ## What the oil promise reveals

The oil story occupies an unusual place in São Tomé and Príncipe's history of contested claims. It was not a classic hoax involving forged evidence or invented events. Geological prospects were genuine, and exploration companies did not simply fabricate the possibility of oil. Yet public narratives frequently moved beyond what the evidence justified.

The episode shows how anticipation can become a kind of political illusion. Once enough people accept a future outcome as inevitable, decisions begin to be made as though it has already happened. Governments plan around expected revenues. Political rivals compete for control of wealth that does not yet exist. Citizens imagine dramatic improvements in living standards. Investors and intermediaries position themselves for opportunities that may never materialise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scienceopen.com/hosted-document?doi=10.1080%2F03056240802411156" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scienceopen.com">[scienceopen.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scienceopen.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hosted document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hope &amp; Oil: Expectations in São Tomé e Príncipeby G Weszkalnys · 2008 · Cited by 87 — Similarly, a relatively non-violent coup...</span></span></span>
