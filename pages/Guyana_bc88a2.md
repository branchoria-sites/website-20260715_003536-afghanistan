## Introduction

These cases are not all the same. El Dorado grew from Indigenous traditions, European misunderstanding and calculated promotion rather than one identifiable inventor. Electoral rigging was organised political fraud. Jonestown combined false miracles, propaganda, rehearsed performances and coercive control. Recent Ponzi and deepfake schemes are straightforward attempts to obtain money by manufacturing credibility. Together, they show that deception succeeds less through national gullibility than through familiar pressures: trust in authority, hope of sudden wealth, fear of enemies, weak access to independent evidence and the persuasive power of apparently respectable institutions.

## El Dorado: the golden empire that sold Guiana

The most famous false claim associated with the wider Guiana region is that a fabulously rich city or empire called Manoa stood beside a vast inland lake, usually named Lake Parime. It became one of the great geographical mirages of the early modern Atlantic world.

El Dorado did not begin simply as a fabricated city. Earlier Spanish accounts had transformed reports of a gold-covered ruler and ceremonial offerings in the northern Andes into a movable legend of immense treasure. As expeditions repeatedly failed, the imagined location shifted eastwards. By the late sixteenth century, European mapmakers and adventurers were placing Manoa deep within Guiana, the broad region encompassing parts of present-day Guyana, Venezuela, Suriname, French Guiana and northern Brazil.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/el-dorado" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalgeographic.com">[National Geographic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalgeographic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">el dorado</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National GeographicThe real history behind El Dorado, the legendary city of gold21 Jan 2017 — English courtier Sir Walter Raleigh made tw...</span></span></span>

Sir Walter Raleigh gave the story its most influential English form. After travelling through Trinidad and the Orinoco basin in 1595, he published *The Discovery of Guiana*, whose full title promised an account of the “great and golden city of Manoa”. The book mixed genuine observations of rivers, communities, politics and natural resources with poorly supported claims about a rich empire awaiting English intervention. The Royal Collection describes Raleigh’s journey as an attempt to find the already fabled El Dorado and restore his position at court. A modern scholarly analysis characterises his book as both promotional and defensive: it was designed to answer critics and persuade potential backers that another expedition was worthwhile.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.public-library.uk/ebooks/42/81.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: public-library.uk">[public-library.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">public-library.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Public LibraryThe Discovery of Guianaby W Raleigh · Cited by 104 — The Discovery of the large, rich, and beautiful EMPIRE Of GUIANA; with...</span></span></span>

That commercial and political purpose matters. Raleigh had returned without the spectacular treasure needed to justify his venture. His account therefore had to convert absence into promise. Testimony from Indigenous informants, Spanish rumours, samples of mineral-bearing rock and assertions about inaccessible territory were arranged to suggest that decisive proof lay just beyond the distance travelled. The narrative offered investors a convenient explanation for failure: the empire was real, but the expedition had lacked sufficient time, manpower and royal support.

The claim was persuasive because it fitted several established expectations. Spanish conquests in Mexico and Peru had already demonstrated that American empires could possess enormous quantities of precious metal. Guyana and the neighbouring Guiana Shield really do contain gold. European readers therefore encountered not a wholly impossible fantasy, but an exaggerated conclusion built around genuine mineral wealth, uncertain geography and reports filtered through translation and colonial rivalry.

Maps helped harden the speculation into apparent fact. Once Lake Parime and Manoa appeared on printed maps, later writers could cite cartography as though it were independent evidence, even when the maps repeated the same unverified stories. Exploration in the nineteenth century eventually removed the supposed lake and city from serious geography. One explanation is that seasonal flooding across the Rupununi savannahs contributed to reports of a great inland body of water. No golden metropolis was found.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raleigh%27s_El_Dorado_expedition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Raleigh&#x27;s El Dorado expedition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Raleigh&#x27;s El Dorado expedition</span></span></span>

Calling El Dorado a simple “hoax” is therefore too neat. Raleigh may have exaggerated strategically, but he probably also believed that rich deposits and an organised inland power existed. The episode sits on the boundary between sincere error, imperial propaganda and investment promotion. Its lasting lesson is that a claim can survive repeated failure when every failed search is treated not as disproof but as evidence that the prize lies slightly farther away.

## When fabricated votes became political reality

Guyana’s most important history of documentary deception concerns elections under the People’s National Congress government of Forbes Burnham. Here the issue was not folklore or misunderstanding. It was the creation of official results that did not reliably reflect the electorate.

The background reaches into the final years of British Guiana. Declassified United States records show that Cold War fears about the left-wing government of Cheddi Jagan led Washington to support covert political action. The National Security Archive describes a campaign involving money, labour activity, propaganda and assistance to opposition forces before the 1964 election. Later US records also document covert support for Burnham’s PNC.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/intelligence/2020-04-06/cia-covert-operations-overthrow-cheddi-jagan-british-guiana-1964" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cia covert operations overthrow cheddi jagan british guiana 1964</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kennedy to approve a covert CIA political campaign to rig national elections in British Guiana, then a British colony but soon to be...R...</span></span></span>

Foreign interference and domestic election fraud should not be collapsed into one claim. The 1964 result occurred under a new proportional representation system and brought Burnham to office through a coalition, although Jagan’s party remained the largest. The more direct case of manufactured electoral legitimacy came in 1968, Guyana’s first general election after independence.

The central device was the overseas vote. Lists supposedly representing Guyanese electors abroad contained names and addresses that investigators could test. Granada Television’s *The Trail of the Vanishing Voters* examined registrations in Britain and reported fictitious or untraceable voters. Contemporary investigations compared addresses with electoral, immigration and population records, turning an abstract accusation of “rigging” into a checkable question: did the people recorded on the list actually exist where the documents said they did?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Guyanese_general_election" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1968 Guyanese general election</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1968 Guyanese general election</span></span></span>

The official result gave the PNC 30 of 53 parliamentary seats and freed Burnham from dependence on his coalition partner. Historical reference works and later scholarship have treated the election as fraudulent, citing invented overseas voters, state control of the process and intimidation. US intelligence assessments subsequently referred candidly to wide-scale rigging and manipulation of overseas votes as expected features of Guyanese elections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve11p1/d362" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[state.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on state.gov.</span></span></span>

The deception worked because the falsehood was embedded in administrative procedure. A fabricated newspaper report can be challenged as a rumour; an invented voter placed on an official register acquires the authority of paperwork. The apparent precision of names, addresses, totals and constituency calculations can make a fraudulent process look bureaucratically normal.

Electoral manipulation continued through the 1970s and 1980s. A Human Rights Watch report published in 1990 concluded that systematic fraud since independence had repeatedly denied Guyanese voters a genuine choice. The disputed 1978 constitutional referendum offers an especially stark illustration: the government announced an approval rate of roughly 97 per cent and turnout above 70 per cent, while opposition estimates placed participation dramatically lower. The result enabled Parliament to avoid an expected election and recast itself as a constituent assembly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hrw.org/reports/pdfs/g/guyana/guyana90o.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hrw.org">[Human Rights Watch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hrw.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hrw.org.</span></span></span>

This history also explains why accusations of election theft carry unusual emotional force in modern Guyana. Yet it is important not to assume that every disputed election is equivalent to the documented frauds of the Burnham era. The useful sceptical standard is evidence: transparent counts, authentic records, credible observation and procedures that allow results to be independently checked.

## Jonestown’s manufactured paradise

The Peoples Temple settlement in north-western Guyana was built around one of the twentieth century’s deadliest systems of deception. Jim Jones presented the agricultural community as a racially integrated, self-sufficient refuge from persecution in the United States. Many followers arrived expecting safety, equality and purposeful communal life. What developed was an isolated settlement marked by surveillance, punishment, exhausting work, restricted movement and Jones’s increasingly paranoid control.

The deception began before Guyana. Jones had cultivated authority through fraudulent or theatrically manipulated displays of religious power, including supposed healings and clairvoyant knowledge. The broader organisation combined social activism that attracted sincere supporters with a leadership culture that demanded escalating loyalty.

Distance strengthened the illusion. Jonestown’s remoteness made independent inspection difficult, while communications with relatives could be supervised. Visitors could be shown energetic performances, communal meals and residents apparently testifying to their happiness. According to accounts later supported by recovered recordings, members rehearsed how to persuade Congressman Leo Ryan’s delegation that conditions were satisfactory. During the November 1978 visit, defectors secretly passed messages asking for help, breaking the staged picture of voluntary contentment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/history/cases-and-criminals/jonestown" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fbi.gov.</span></span></span>

Jones also manufactured repeated crises. He told residents that hostile governments, racists, mercenaries or defectors were preparing to destroy the community. During “White Night” exercises, members were summoned for siege drills and discussions of collective death. Survivors described occasions when people were given drinks said to contain poison, only to be told afterwards that the episode had been a loyalty test. These rehearsals blurred performance and threat: each false emergency trained residents to respond more automatically to the next one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcnews.com/US/40-years-jonestown-massacre-members-describe-jim-jones/story?id=57933856" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcnews.com">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ABC News40 years after Jonestown massacre, ex-members describe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC News40 years after Jonestown massacre, ex-members describe</span></span></span>

The deception collapsed on 18 November 1978. Ryan’s party prepared to leave with residents who wished to defect. Temple gunmen attacked them at the Port Kaituma airstrip, killing Ryan and four others. At Jonestown, more than 900 people died in a cyanide poisoning ordered by Jones. The FBI investigation involved survivor interviews, forensic identification and examination of the Temple’s extensive archive of recordings and documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/history/cases-and-criminals/jonestown" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fbi.gov.</span></span></span>

The familiar phrase “mass suicide” conceals the degree of coercion. Children could not consent, some adults were restrained or injected, armed guards were present, and residents had been subjected to isolation and psychological control. For that reason, many historians, survivors and later documentaries use “mass murder-suicide” or emphasise mass murder rather than treating every death as a voluntary ideological act.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/999815cf150f8a0b04f32b009c6f5641" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apnews.com.</span></span></span>

Jonestown later generated a second layer of contested claims. Theories alleged that the settlement was a CIA experiment, that foreign troops killed the residents, or that official agencies concealed a different death toll or motive. Scholarly work on these theories finds that inconsistencies in early reports, poor record-keeping and genuine government failures created openings for speculation. None of that establishes the sweeping claims. The largest body of evidence remains Jones’s own recordings, witness testimony, physical evidence and organisational documents, which reveal an internally directed programme of deception, coercion and killing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358415202_Reflections_on_Jonestown_Conspiracy_Theories_or_Inept_Record_Keeping" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) Reflections on Jonestown Conspiracy Theories or</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) Reflections on Jonestown Conspiracy Theories or</span></span></span>

The case should not be treated as evidence that Guyana itself produced or endorsed the fraud. The overwhelming majority of the dead were Americans, and the organisation had imported its leadership and internal culture from the United States. Guyana mattered because Jones exploited remoteness, limited scrutiny and the government’s interest in agricultural settlement to build a controlled information environment.

## The promise of effortless wealth

The mechanics of El Dorado reappeared in modern financial form in the Accelerated Capital Firm Inc case. The company promoted foreign-exchange trading as the source of unusually attractive returns. Guyanese authorities said that thousands of people placed roughly US$20 million with the operation in 2020; contemporary reports put the number of clients at around 17,000.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ccac.gov.gy/blog/2020/08/27/govt-working-to-recover-monies-invested-in-ponzi-scheme/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ccac.gov.gy">[ccac.gov.gy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ccac.gov.gy</span><span class="citation-popover-title">t working to recover monies invested in ponzi scheme</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">t working to recover monies invested in ponzi scheme</span></span></span>

A Ponzi scheme creates the appearance of successful investment by using money from newer participants to pay earlier ones. Initial payouts become powerful advertising. Recipients may show friends real cash or account statements, making personal testimony more persuasive than warnings from regulators. The scheme fails when withdrawals and promised returns exceed incoming funds. Guyana’s Securities Council identifies promises of large, quick and low-risk profits as core warning signs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.guyanasecuritiescouncil.com/pozischeme.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: guyanasecuritiescouncil.com">[guyanasecuritiescouncil.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">guyanasecuritiescouncil.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on guyanasecuritiescouncil.com.</span></span></span>

The Accelerated Capital case also illustrates how modern fraud borrows the language of legitimate finance. “Foreign exchange”, digital dashboards and corporate registration can sound technical and regulated even when investors have no independent proof that trades are occurring. Authorities stated that the firm lacked the licences required to operate as a financial institution. Its operators were eventually convicted in 2026 of illegally running the scheme, after years of investigations and numerous charges; associated money-laundering convictions were also secured against agents involved in handling funds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arin-carib.org/ponzi-scheme-agents-jailed-4-years-for-money-laundering/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arin-carib.org">[ARIN Caribbean]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arin-carib.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ARIN Caribbean Ponzi Scheme agents jailed 4 years for money laundering</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ARIN Caribbean Ponzi Scheme agents jailed 4 years for money laundering</span></span></span>

The persuasive context was not merely greed. Low returns on ordinary savings, limited access to sophisticated investments and visible early payouts can make such offers appear to be rare opportunities rather than obvious impossibilities. Social trust does much of the recruiting: people join because relatives, colleagues or respected local figures say they have already profited.

## From forged paperwork to synthetic authority

Guyana’s newer scams increasingly imitate people rather than documents. Authorities and public officials have warned of manipulated videos using artificial intelligence to make political figures appear to endorse investments or financial offers. One publicly flagged example used an AI-generated likeness and cloned voice of President Irfaan Ali to promote a fraudulent scheme. The video’s purpose was not primarily political satire; it attempted to transfer the authority of the presidency to an unrelated commercial solicitation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.facebook.com/presidentirfaanaligy/posts/%EF%B8%8F-public-notice-%EF%B8%8Fit-has-been-brought-to-our-attention-that-an-ai-generated-video/1413353826824834/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: facebook.com">[Facebook]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">facebook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on facebook.com.</span></span></span>

Deepfakes improve an old fraud technique. Earlier scammers forged letters, newspaper pages, signatures or websites. Synthetic video can combine a familiar face, plausible voice and copied news graphics in a form that feels like direct personal evidence. Research into deepfake impersonation notes that readily available face and voice tools are now used for scams and political disinformation, while compression and reposting can make visual defects harder for ordinary viewers to identify.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cetas.turing.ac.uk/publications/deepfake-scams-poisoned-chatbots" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cetas.turing.ac.uk">[Emerging Tech &amp; Security Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cetas.turing.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">deepfake scams poisoned chatbots</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">deepfake scams poisoned chatbots</span></span></span>

The most reliable test is therefore not whether a clip “looks real”. It is whether the claimed announcement appears through the official channels of the institution supposedly making it, whether the investment is registered, and whether independent reporting confirms it. Urgency, guaranteed returns, private messaging and requests to transfer money are stronger fraud indicators than imperfect lip movement.

## What Guyana’s deception stories have in common

The most memorable Guyana-linked deceptions differ greatly in scale, motive and responsibility, but they repeatedly exploit the same gaps between appearance and verification.

**Real details support a larger fiction.** Guiana contains gold, but not Raleigh’s golden empire. Jonestown contained farms, schools and committed idealists, but not the free paradise presented to outsiders. Ponzi investors sometimes received genuine early payments, but those payments did not prove profitable trading.

**Authority is borrowed.** Maps made Manoa look geographically established. Electoral registers made invented voters look administratively real. Jim Jones borrowed the language of religion, socialism and racial justice. Investment schemes use corporate terminology, while deepfakes borrow the face and voice of public office.

**Isolation protects the claim.** El Dorado was always beyond the explored river. Jonestown physically restricted access and controlled communication. Financial fraud hides transactions behind technical explanations. Online impersonation moves victims quickly from public posts into private channels.

**Exposure requires evidence that can be checked independently.** Explorers tested the supposed inland lake against geography. Journalists investigated overseas voter addresses. Defectors, recordings and forensic work revealed conditions at Jonestown. Regulators followed licences, deposits and payment flows in investment cases.

The central lesson is not that Guyanese history is unusually full of hoaxes. It is that Guyana has repeatedly stood at points where powerful stories met difficult verification: a little-known interior advertised to European investors, election systems hidden behind state paperwork, an isolated commune shielded from scrutiny and digital scams able to cross borders instantly. In each case, the deception weakened when outsiders could compare the persuasive story with physical places, identifiable people, original records and traceable money.
