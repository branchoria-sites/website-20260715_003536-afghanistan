## Introduction

[Brunei]({{ 'how-false-claims-gained-authority-in/' | relative_url }})’s approach to false claims sits at the intersection of public order, digital misinformation and freedom of expression. Unlike countries that have adopted dedicated “fake news” statutes, Brunei relies largely on existing provisions in the Public Order Act, official corrections and, more recently, public fact-checking initiatives. The central question is not whether false information can cause harm—it plainly can during health emergencies, scam campaigns or public-safety incidents—but when a false claim should become a criminal matter rather than a mistake, rumour or disputed interpretation. The answer has become increasingly important as misleading messages circulate rapidly through social media, messaging apps and manipulated screenshots that imitate official announcements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.agc.gov.bn/AGC%20Images/LAWS/ACT_PDF/cap148.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: agc.gov.bn">[Attorney General&#x27;s Chamber Brunei]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">agc.gov.bn</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Attorney General&#x27;s Chamber Bruneilaws of brunei chapter 148 public order</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">False reports and public mischief. 34. Dissemination of false report. 35. Public mischief. Abetment and failure to report...Read more...</span></span></span>

For a country whose most visible modern encounters with deception involve rumours, scams and fabricated online notices rather than famous historical hoaxes, the debate is less about spectacular frauds and more about how authorities distinguish harmful falsehoods from ordinary error. The evolution from prosecution alone towards verification platforms such as Waspada.bn reveals an important shift in Brunei’s response to contested truth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thescoop.co/2025/06/05/aiti-launches-fact-checking-platform-to-curb-misinformation-fake-news/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thescoop.co">[The Scoop]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thescoop.co</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Scoop AITI launches fact-checking platform to curb misinformation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The ScoopAITI launches fact-checking platform to curb misinformation...June 5, 2025 — 5 Jun 2025 — Brunei&#x27;s internet regulator has launc...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 5, 2025</span></span></span>

## What Section 34 Targets

The legal centrepiece of Brunei’s response is Section 34 of the Public Order Act, which falls under a section of the law dealing with “false reports and public mischief”. The provision addresses the dissemination of false reports or statements likely to create public alarm, anxiety or disruption. Official guidance and public warnings routinely cite the possibility of fines and imprisonment for those who knowingly spread such information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.agc.gov.bn/AGC%20Images/LAWS/ACT_PDF/cap148.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: agc.gov.bn">[agc.gov.bn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">agc.gov.bn</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Attorney General&#x27;s Chamber Bruneilaws of brunei chapter 148 public order</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">False reports and public mischief. 34. Dissemination of false report. 35. Public mischief. Abetment and failure to report...Read more...</span></span></span>

The rationale is straightforward. False reports about disease outbreaks, public emergencies, criminal incidents or government actions can trigger panic, damage reputations or divert official resources. In a relatively small and closely connected society, a viral message claiming police action, a health threat or an official directive may spread quickly and influence behaviour before authorities have time to respond.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://borneobulletin.com.bn/spreading-false-news-is-serious-offence-says-information-dept/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: borneobulletin.com.bn">[borneobulletin.com.bn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">borneobulletin.com.bn</span><span class="citation-popover-title">spreading false news is serious offence says information dept</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spreading false news is serious offence, says Information...5 Aug 2022 — Those spreading false information can be charged under Section...</span></span></span>

Yet the practical challenge lies in defining the boundary between:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Deliberate fabrication**, where someone invents a claim or knowingly circulates a falsehood.
* **Reckless sharing**, where accuracy is ignored.
* **Misunderstanding or error**, where a person believes the information is true.
* **Good-faith discussion**, where facts remain uncertain or disputed.

</div>

These distinctions matter because misinformation rarely appears in a pure form. A message may contain a genuine photograph, a real document or a partially accurate event while drawing misleading conclusions from it. Criminal law is often most effective against intentional deception but becomes more controversial when applied to ambiguous or evolving information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rune.une.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/df58ff6f-b130-4a1b-95e8-a0e8418bfe81/content" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rune.une.edu.au">[Rune]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rune.une.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rune&#x27;FAKE NEWS&#x27; IN ASEAN: LEGISLATIVE RESPONSESJanuary 26, 2022 — by RB Smith · 2021 · Cited by 26 — There are two offences: (a) Spreadin...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 26, 2022</span></span></span>

## Prosecution, Correction and Proportionality

The tension between public protection and free expression becomes most visible in enforcement cases. During the COVID-19 period, authorities pursued action against some individuals accused of spreading false information. One widely reported case involved allegations that false claims had been made regarding quarantine-order violations and police activity. Charges were reportedly brought under Section 34 of the Public Order Act, illustrating the government's willingness to treat certain misinformation as a public-order issue rather than merely a communications problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://monitor.civicus.org/explore/woman-charged-false-statement-covid-19-brunei-maintains-harsh-restrictions-freedoms/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: monitor.civicus.org">[Civicus Monitor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">monitor.civicus.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Civicus MonitorWoman charged for false statement on COVID-19 as...27 Sept 2022 — Hajah Faizah binti Haji Abdul Gapar was charged under S...</span></span></span>

Supporters of such enforcement argue that false alarms can produce tangible harm. During a health crisis, inaccurate reports about infections, lockdowns or official actions may influence thousands of people within hours. In that view, deterrence serves a protective function, especially when rumours appear designed to mislead or provoke fear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://monitor.civicus.org/explore/woman-charged-false-statement-covid-19-brunei-maintains-harsh-restrictions-freedoms/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: monitor.civicus.org">[Civicus Monitor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">monitor.civicus.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Civicus MonitorWoman charged for false statement on COVID-19 as...27 Sept 2022 — Hajah Faizah binti Haji Abdul Gapar was charged under S...</span></span></span>

Critics, however, point to a recurring problem seen across Southeast Asia and beyond: laws aimed at combating false information can be drafted broadly enough that uncertainty emerges over what counts as a punishable falsehood. Legal scholars and free-expression advocates have frequently warned that anti-false-information measures may discourage legitimate criticism, investigative reporting or public debate if people fear legal consequences for sharing information later judged inaccurate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://verfassungsblog.de/abstract-panic-on-fake-news-fear-and-freedom-in-southeast-asia/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: verfassungsblog.de">[verfassungsblog.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">verfassungsblog.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on verfassungsblog.de.</span></span></span>

The proportionality question therefore becomes crucial. A forged government notice created to deceive is different from an ordinary citizen forwarding an alarming message believed to be genuine. Likewise, a malicious scam promotion differs from a mistaken interpretation of an unfolding event. The more severe the potential penalty, the more important these distinctions become.

## Why Fact-Checking Became Part of the Solution

A purely punitive approach has obvious limits. Authorities can prosecute some cases, but prosecutions occur after misinformation has already circulated. The practical challenge is reducing the spread of false claims before they become widely accepted.

This is the context in which Brunei launched Waspada.bn, a public fact-checking initiative designed to verify circulating claims and provide authoritative clarification. Announced in 2025 by the Authority for Info-communications Technology Industry (AITI), the platform was presented as a way for citizens to check information shared through social media and messaging channels. Officials described it as part of a broader effort to improve public resilience against misinformation, scams and fabricated news.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thescoop.co/2025/06/05/aiti-launches-fact-checking-platform-to-curb-misinformation-fake-news/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thescoop.co">[thescoop.co]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thescoop.co</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Scoop AITI launches fact-checking platform to curb misinformation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The ScoopAITI launches fact-checking platform to curb misinformation...June 5, 2025 — 5 Jun 2025 — Brunei&#x27;s internet regulator has launc...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 5, 2025</span></span></span>

The significance of the platform extends beyond individual rumours. It represents a different governance model:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Correction rather than punishment** as the first response.
* **Public verification** instead of relying solely on legal deterrence.
* **Media literacy and awareness** alongside enforcement.
* **Transparency about disputed claims** through a central reference point.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://waspada.bn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: waspada.bn">[waspada.bn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">waspada.bn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Brunei&#x27;s Trusted Fact-Checking PlatformWaspada.bn is Brunei&#x27;s trusted fact-checking platform dedicated to identifying misinformation, cla...</span></span></span>

</div>

This shift recognises a basic reality of modern misinformation. False claims often spread faster than official investigations. The most effective response may therefore be rapid clarification rather than waiting for a criminal case to unfold.

## Waspada.bn and the Shift Towards Public Verification

The emergence of Waspada.bn also reflects a broader change in how governments address online deception. Earlier responses frequently focused on identifying offenders and imposing penalties. Newer approaches increasingly combine enforcement with public participation in verification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thescoop.co/2025/06/05/aiti-launches-fact-checking-platform-to-curb-misinformation-fake-news/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thescoop.co">[The Scoop]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thescoop.co</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Scoop AITI launches fact-checking platform to curb misinformation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The ScoopAITI launches fact-checking platform to curb misinformation...June 5, 2025 — 5 Jun 2025 — Brunei&#x27;s internet regulator has launc...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 5, 2025</span></span></span>

For readers interested in the history of hoaxes and misinformation, this is an important development. Traditional hoax exposures relied on journalists, scientists, investigators or sceptics uncovering evidence after a false story had spread. Modern fact-checking systems attempt to shorten that timeline by publishing corrections while the claim is still circulating.

In Brunei’s case, the platform's purpose is not only to debunk individual rumours but also to encourage a habit of checking before sharing. That matters because many contemporary falsehoods do not originate as elaborate conspiracies. ## When Should a False Claim Become a Crime?

Brunei’s experience highlights a question faced by many countries confronting digital misinformation: not every false statement is equally harmful, and not every error should attract criminal sanctions.

A workable distinction often rests on several factors:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Intent:** Was the claim knowingly fabricated?
* **Harm:** Did it create a realistic risk of panic, fraud or public danger?
* **Context:** Was the information presented as fact, speculation or opinion?
* **Correction:** Did the person retract or clarify the claim once errors became clear?
* **Scale:** Was the dissemination likely to affect large numbers of people?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rune.une.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/df58ff6f-b130-4a1b-95e8-a0e8418bfe81/content" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rune.une.edu.au">[Rune]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rune.une.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rune&#x27;FAKE NEWS&#x27; IN ASEAN: LEGISLATIVE RESPONSESJanuary 26, 2022 — by RB Smith · 2021 · Cited by 26 — There are two offences: (a) Spreadin...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 26, 2022</span></span></span>

Brunei’s current framework combines legal sanctions for serious false reports with an increasing emphasis on verification and public education. That combination reflects an implicit recognition that misinformation is not only a law-enforcement problem. It is also an information problem.
