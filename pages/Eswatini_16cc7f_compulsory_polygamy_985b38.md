## Introduction

One of the most persistent false stories associated with [Eswatini]({{ 'which-eswatini-stories-were-actually/' | relative_url }}) claimed that King Mswati III had ordered all men to take multiple wives or face punishment. Over time, the rumour changed shape. What began as a fabricated royal decree about compulsory polygamy later reappeared as an equally fictional immigration programme inviting foreign men to move to Eswatini and marry local women. None of these claims were true, but they spread widely because they attached invented policies to a real and highly visible fact: the king himself practises polygamy. Fact-checkers, government officials and journalists repeatedly debunked the story, yet it continued to resurface in new forms for years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pesacheck.org/false-men-in-eswatini-have-not-been-ordered-to-marry-more-wives-or-face-jail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pesacheck.org">[Pesacheck]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pesacheck.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">false men in eswatini have not been ordered to marry more wives or face jail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FALSE: Men in eSwatini have not been ordered to &#x27;marry...16 May 2019 — The defining characteristic of these rumours on social m...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2019</span></span></span>

## What the supposed royal decree claimed

The best-known version emerged in 2019. Articles and social-media posts claimed that King Mswati III had decreed that every man in Eswatini would be required to marry at least two wives, with some versions increasing the number to five. Men who failed to comply were supposedly threatened with imprisonment. Other variants claimed the government would help finance weddings, provide housing, or otherwise support the expansion of polygamous households.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pesacheck.org/false-men-in-eswatini-have-not-been-ordered-to-marry-more-wives-or-face-jail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pesacheck.org">[pesacheck.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pesacheck.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">false men in eswatini have not been ordered to marry more wives or face jail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FALSE: Men in eSwatini have not been ordered to &#x27;marry...16 May 2019 — The defining characteristic of these rumours on social m...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2019</span></span></span>

The stories typically included details designed to make them look official. They referred to a specific implementation date, cited unnamed authorities and presented the measure as a response to social problems. These details gave the appearance of a genuine government announcement while avoiding the evidence that a real law would generate, such as legislation, parliamentary records or formal government notices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pesacheck.org/false-men-in-eswatini-have-not-been-ordered-to-marry-more-wives-or-face-jail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pesacheck.org">[Pesacheck]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pesacheck.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">false men in eswatini have not been ordered to marry more wives or face jail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FALSE: Men in eSwatini have not been ordered to &#x27;marry...16 May 2019 — The defining characteristic of these rumours on social m...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2019</span></span></span>

## How fact-checkers traced and disproved it

The story spread through online news sites and social-media sharing, with one widely circulated version traced to reports reproduced by several websites across Africa. As the claim gained attention, the Eswatini government publicly rejected it. Officials described the reports as false and damaging to both the monarchy and the country.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.africanews.com/2019/05/15/fake-news-eswatini-angered-by-royal-order-polygamy-story/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: africanews.com">[africanews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">africanews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fake news eswatini angered by royal order polygamy story</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Swazi men would have to marry several wives starting from June... Fake news: eSwatini angered by royal order polygamy story. Fake news...</span></span></span>

Independent fact-checking organisations reached the same conclusion. Investigations by AFP Fact Check, PesaCheck and other outlets found no evidence that any such decree had been issued. Researchers could not locate legislation, government announcements or official documents supporting the claim. Nor could they identify any mechanism by which the supposed policy was being implemented.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pesacheck.org/false-men-in-eswatini-have-not-been-ordered-to-marry-more-wives-or-face-jail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pesacheck.org">[Pesacheck]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pesacheck.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">false men in eswatini have not been ordered to marry more wives or face jail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FALSE: Men in eSwatini have not been ordered to &#x27;marry...16 May 2019 — The defining characteristic of these rumours on social m...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2019</span></span></span>

The debunking process highlighted several warning signs common to viral misinformation:

* **No verifiable source.** Reports relied on anonymous or unattributed statements.
* **No official documentation.** A policy of such magnitude would leave a substantial public record.
* **Contradictory versions.** Different retellings disagreed on how many wives were required and what penalties would apply.
* **Replication without verification.** Many websites copied the claim from earlier reports rather than independently confirming it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pesacheck.org/false-men-in-eswatini-have-not-been-ordered-to-marry-more-wives-or-face-jail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pesacheck.org">[pesacheck.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pesacheck.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">false men in eswatini have not been ordered to marry more wives or face jail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FALSE: Men in eSwatini have not been ordered to &#x27;marry...16 May 2019 — The defining characteristic of these rumours on social m...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2019</span></span></span>

The speed with which the rumour spread illustrated how sensational stories can circulate internationally before fact-checks reach comparable audiences.

## Why the hoax sounded believable

The durability of the story owed less to convincing evidence than to cultural assumptions held by many readers outside Eswatini.

King Mswati III's marriages have long attracted international media attention. Stories about royal weddings, traditional ceremonies and the monarch's household are frequently reported abroad. Because these accounts are genuine, they created a foundation on which fabricated claims could be built.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mswati_III" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mswati III</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mswati III</span></span></span>

The hoax also exploited stereotypes about African monarchies. Readers unfamiliar with Eswatini's institutions could imagine an all-powerful king imposing personal preferences on the population. The claim therefore required little prior knowledge to seem plausible.

Another factor was the inclusion of a social explanation. Some versions implied that compulsory polygamy would solve demographic or family-related problems. Such justifications gave the false decree an appearance of policy logic, even though no evidence supported the underlying claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pesacheck.org/false-men-in-eswatini-have-not-been-ordered-to-marry-more-wives-or-face-jail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pesacheck.org">[Pesacheck]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pesacheck.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">false men in eswatini have not been ordered to marry more wives or face jail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FALSE: Men in eSwatini have not been ordered to &#x27;marry...16 May 2019 — The defining characteristic of these rumours on social m...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2019</span></span></span>

## Why the story returned in new forms

Most hoaxes fade after exposure. This one evolved.

By the early 2020s, a new version began circulating online. Instead of forcing Swazi men to marry more wives, the story claimed that Eswatini faced a severe shortage of men and was therefore inviting foreign men to immigrate, receive citizenship, marry multiple Swazi women and enjoy government support. ## What the mutations reveal about misinformation

The compulsory-polygamy rumour is notable not because it fooled experts, but because it survived repeated debunking. Each new version preserved the elements that had made the original successful while discarding details that had already been exposed.

The pattern is common in modern misinformation. A claim rarely survives because its evidence is strong. Instead, it survives because it satisfies expectations, prejudices or curiosities already held by an audience. In this case, real knowledge about the king's polygamous household became the raw material for increasingly elaborate inventions.
