## Introduction

Antigua’s most famous disputed stories are not elaborate frauds or sensational hoaxes. Instead, they are tourism slogans, inherited historical misunderstandings and local legends that have been repeated so often that many visitors assume they are established facts. The claim that Antigua has exactly 365 beaches, the story that St John’s Cathedral stands on a volcano, and the long-standing belief that the island’s first inhabitants were the Siboney all occupy a grey area between folklore, marketing and historical error. These stories matter because they show how attractive, memorable claims can outlive the evidence behind them and become part of a destination’s identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cpoise.gov.ag/2023/01/03/new-year-old-myths/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cpoise.gov.ag">[cpoise.gov.ag]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cpoise.gov.ag</span><span class="citation-popover-title">New Year. ## The Claim of Exactly 365 Beaches

No tourism slogan is more closely associated with Antigua than the assertion that it has “365 beaches, one for every day of the year”. The phrase appears in tourism literature, travel writing and promotional campaigns, and it has become one of the island’s defining brands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.foratravel.com/guides/THMS2X/antigua-and-barbuda-the-365-beaches-are-just-the-beginning-jie-xu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foratravel.com">[Fora]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foratravel.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ForaAntigua &amp; Barbuda: The 365 Beaches Are Just...With 365 beaches in Antigua and Barbuda, we say there&#x27;s a different beach for every da...</span></span></span>

The difficulty is that nobody can point to a definitive count. Beaches are not fixed objects that can be measured as neatly as houses or roads. Small coves merge into larger stretches of sand, seasonal changes alter shorelines, and different surveys use different definitions of what qualifies as a separate beach. Even local commentators have acknowledged that no universally accepted counting exercise appears to exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theantiguan.com/post/who-counted-antigua-s-365-beaches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theantiguan.com">[The Antiguan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theantiguan.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">who counted antigua s 365 beaches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The AntiguanWho Counted Antigua&#x27;s 365 Beaches?13 Dec 2021 — Who Counted Antigua&#x27;s 365 Beaches?... No one can remember. It&#x27;s our best kep...</span></span></span>

That does not mean the slogan is entirely false. Antigua genuinely possesses an unusually large number of beaches relative to its size, thanks to its deeply indented coastline, bays and natural harbours. The figure of 365 functions more as a symbolic marketing device than a verified geographical statistic. It is memorable, easy to repeat and perfectly suited to tourism advertising.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigua" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The story survives because it captures a truth while exaggerating its precision. Visitors quickly discover that Antigua does have beaches almost everywhere they travel on the island. Whether the total is exactly 365 is less important to tourism promoters than the image the number creates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.foratravel.com/guides/THMS2X/antigua-and-barbuda-the-365-beaches-are-just-the-beginning-jie-xu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foratravel.com">[Fora]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foratravel.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ForaAntigua &amp; Barbuda: The 365 Beaches Are Just...With 365 beaches in Antigua and Barbuda, we say there&#x27;s a different beach for every da...</span></span></span>

## Did St John's Cathedral Really Stand on a Volcano?

Another enduring local legend claims that St John’s Cathedral was built on top of a volcano. The story often appears in casual conversation and tourism lore, perhaps because the cathedral occupies a prominent hill overlooking the capital and because Antigua itself has volcanic origins.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cpoise.gov.ag/2023/01/03/new-year-old-myths/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cpoise.gov.ag">[cpoise.gov.ag]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cpoise.gov.ag</span><span class="citation-popover-title">New Year. Old Myths?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antigua and Barbuda3 Jan 2023 — The first Antiguans were the Siboney. Claim: It was commonly held that the first people who lived on Anti...</span></span></span>

The reality is more complicated. Antigua is indeed a volcanic island in geological terms, and volcanic rock can be found across parts of the island. Historical buildings, including structures in the Nelson’s Dockyard area, were constructed using locally quarried volcanic stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.facebook.com/nelsonsdockyardnationalpark/posts/did-you-know-that-these-buildings-were-made-from-volcanic-stone-found-here-in-an/10160900919313824/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: facebook.com">[Facebook]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">facebook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Did you know that these buildings were made from...Did you know that these buildings were made from volcanic stone found here in...</span></span></span>

However, there is no accepted geological evidence that the cathedral hill is the cone of a volcano or that the cathedral itself sits atop a distinct volcanic crater. The cathedral’s documented history focuses on successive church buildings destroyed by earthquakes and rebuilt on the same commanding site. Sources describing the cathedral’s development refer to its hilltop location but do not identify the hill as a volcano.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://millreeffund.org/the-cathedral-of-st-john-the-divine/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: millreeffund.org">[millreeffund.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">millreeffund.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Cathedral of St</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">John the DivineThe Cathedral of St. John the Divine is a stunningly beautiful historical landmark that sits on a hilltop in St. John&#x27;s an...</span></span></span>

The myth probably persists because it blends several true elements. Antigua has volcanic geology. The cathedral stands on a dramatic elevation. Earthquakes have affected the building repeatedly. When these facts are combined through generations of retelling, a more dramatic story emerges: a cathedral supposedly perched on a volcano.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://millreeffund.org/the-cathedral-of-st-john-the-divine/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: millreeffund.org">[millreeffund.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">millreeffund.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Cathedral of St</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">John the DivineThe Cathedral of St. John the Divine is a stunningly beautiful historical landmark that sits on a hilltop in St. John&#x27;s an...</span></span></span>

This is a classic example of how local folklore develops. Nobody needs to invent a complete fiction; a collection of loosely connected facts gradually hardens into a vivid narrative.

## How Old Labels Became Accepted History

Perhaps the most significant historical misconception concerns the identity of Antigua’s earliest inhabitants. For decades many books, tourist materials and educational resources stated that the island’s first people were the Siboney, sometimes described as “stone-age” settlers. The claim became so widespread that it appeared in numerous historical summaries and guidebooks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://1997-2001.state.gov/background_notes/antigua_0400_bgn.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: 1997-2001.state.gov">[state.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">1997-2001.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">antigua 0400 bgn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">antigua 0400 bgn</span></span></span>

Modern archaeological research has challenged this interpretation. Archaeologists working in Antigua have argued that the label “Siboney” was incorrectly applied to the island’s earliest known inhabitants. The term is associated with peoples elsewhere in the Caribbean, particularly Cuba, and there is no evidence that a distinct Siboney population occupied Antigua in the way older accounts suggested. Researchers instead use broader terms such as “Archaic People” or “Archaic Age peoples” for the island’s earliest settlers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archaeologyantigua.org/index.php/archaeological-background/common-myths" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeologyantigua.org">[archaeologyantigua.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeologyantigua.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Common MythsThe Siboney versus Archaic Peoples. It is commonly held that the first people who lived on Antigua were the Siboney. The Sibo...</span></span></span>

The persistence of the Siboney story illustrates how historical errors can become self-reinforcing. Once an idea appears in textbooks, museum labels, travel guides and secondary histories, later writers often repeat it without returning to the archaeological evidence. Over time the claim gains authority simply because so many sources repeat it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archaeologyantigua.org/index.php/archaeological-background/common-myths" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeologyantigua.org">[archaeologyantigua.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeologyantigua.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Common MythsThe Siboney versus Archaic Peoples. It is commonly held that the first people who lived on Antigua were the Siboney. The Sibo...</span></span></span>

In Antigua’s case, the correction did not expose a deliberate fraud. Instead, it revealed the long life of inherited assumptions. The mistaken label survived because it was familiar, convenient and rarely questioned.

## Why These Stories Endure

The most enduring Antiguan myths share several characteristics:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* They contain a kernel of truth.
* They are easy to remember.
* They provide a stronger story than a more nuanced explanation.
* They are repeated by trusted sources such as guidebooks, tourism campaigns or educational materials.
